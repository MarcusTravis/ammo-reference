Common questions

What is the best deer cartridge?

Almost any common centerfire deer cartridge will cleanly take a whitetail. What matters most is a bullet built for deer, a cartridge you can shoot well, and shots kept inside your own ethical range. Popular all-around picks on our charts include the .243 Winchester (100 gr, 1,945 ft-lbs, about 300 yd), 6.5 Creedmoor (143 gr, 2,315 ft-lbs, 400–500 yd), .308 Winchester (150 gr, 2,648 ft-lbs, 400 yd) and .30-06 Springfield (165 gr, 2,872 ft-lbs, 400–450 yd). Some states limit deer hunting to straight-wall cartridges such as the .350 Legend. See the beginner's deer cartridge guide.

6.5 Creedmoor or .308 Winchester: which is better?

With typical loads the .308 Winchester is a little faster at the muzzle (2,820 vs 2,700 ft/s) and carries about 14% more energy (2,648 vs 2,315 ft-lbs), which usually means more recoil. The 6.5 Creedmoor's bullet has the higher BC (0.623 vs about 0.31), so it holds speed better and drifts less in the wind at long range. With match loads at 1,000 yd, the 6.5 Creedmoor drops 319.9" and drifts 68.5" in a 10 mph crosswind, against 412.2" and 101.0" for the .308. Both are fine deer and elk rounds. What twist rate do I need?

Twist rate is how many inches of barrel it takes the rifling to spin the bullet one full turn (1:8 = one turn in 8"). Longer, heavier bullets need a faster (smaller number) twist to stay stable. Typical twists on our charts: 6.5 Creedmoor 1:8, .308 Winchester and .30-06 Springfield 1:10, .223 Remington 1:9 (1:7 for 70+ gr bullets), .22 LR 1:16. Every chart and cartridge profile lists the twist for its typical load.

What is ballistic coefficient (BC)?

Ballistic coefficient is how well a bullet cuts through air. A higher number holds speed better, drops less and drifts less in the wind. A sleek, heavy bullet that starts slower can pass a light, fast one downrange. Our charts show the maker's published G1 BC where one exists, and mark typical values with ≈.

What is the difference between MOA and MIL?

Both are angles used to adjust a scope. One MOA is 1.047" at 100 yards. One MIL (milliradian) is 3.6" at 100 yards, so a MIL is a bigger step, about 3.4 MOA. Neither is more accurate; what matters is that your reticle and turrets use the same unit. The precision chart lists drop and wind in inches and MOA.

What is maximum point-blank range (MPBR)?

Maximum point-blank range is the farthest distance you can hold dead-on and stay inside the animal's vital zone. Our charts use a 2" zone for small game, 4" for varmints and 6" for deer-size game. For example, the 6.5 Creedmoor's MPBR on a 6" zone is 275 yd and the .308 Winchester's is 266 yd. MPBR figures are for comparing cartridges; confirm your own drops at the range.

Where do the numbers come from?

Speed, energy and BC are typical figures for a named factory load. Every chart is checked against the maker's published data (or SAAMI and spec sheets where no single maker load applies), and each chart shows the date it was last checked. Real numbers vary by brand, lot and barrel length, so always check the ammo maker's data for your exact load. Every change is listed on the Updates page.
